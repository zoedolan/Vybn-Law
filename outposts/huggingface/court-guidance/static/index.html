Court Guidance · a practical beta for courts adopting AI

How do we design systems that care at least as much as we do about getting things right?

The method: imagine the end state — a court’s own system — then work backward. Four layers, in order.

1 — raw material

Every court already has its raw material: opinions, rules, forms, self-help guides, resource lists. Distill it to the basics — the plain shape of a public library like Public Counsel’s appellate clinic resources — so any court can assemble and verify its own set.

2 — rules / guidelines

Statewide appellate rules and local rules, broken into small records the system must follow: one requirement, one exact source, one owner, one review status. The same text a clerk would check — never the model’s paraphrase.

3 — ethics / values

Lawyers answer to rules of professional conduct; judges, to canons of ethics. The AI answers to a public corollary: written standards for what it may do, what it must refuse, and when it hands off to a person.

4 — self / own system

The end state imagined first: the court’s own system, running on its materials, its rules, its standards, under its control — an adoption plan that ends in a mechanism buttressing the court’s role in administering justice. The assistant a person can talk to is the last thing built, not the first.

Then the cycle repeats: the system returns sharper material, clearer rules, stronger standards.
